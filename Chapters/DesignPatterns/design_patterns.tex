\chapter{Design Patterns}
\label{chap:designpatterns}
\textbf{Design Patterns} describe how to solve recurring design problems 
to design flexible and reusable object-oriented software, that is, 
objects that are easier to implement, change, test, and reuse.

\section{Creational Patterns}
\label{sec:creational}

Creational patterns isolate \emph{how} objects come into existence from the code that uses them.
They matter when constructors alone couple callers to concrete types, or when creation itself is a design concern (families, multi-step builds, clones, unique instances).

%%% %%% FACTORY METHOD %%% %%% %%%
\subsection{Factory Method}
Factory Method lets a creator decide \emph{which} concrete product to build behind a shared product interface.
Callers depend on that interface and on the creator's factory method, not on \texttt{new ConcreteType(...)}.
Subclassing the creator (or overriding a default factory method) is how new product variants plug in.

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
            \hline
            \textbf{What for} & \highlightit{creating objects} without 
                                having to specify the exact class 
                                of the \\ 
                              & object that will be created\\
            \hline
            \textbf{How to} & a \highlightit{factory method} is used 
                              \highlightit{instead of calling a constructor}:
                              it can be \\
                            & either \highlightit{specified in an interface} 
                              and implemented by child classes, \\
                            & or \highlight{implemented in a base class} 
                              and optionally overridden by \\
                            & derived classes\\

            \hline
            \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ exact types and dependencies 
                                   of the objects are unknown \\
                                 & \quad beforehand \\
                                 & $\bullet$ internal components of 
                                   a library or framework need to be 
                                   extended\\
                                 & \quad by users\\ 
                                 & $\bullet$ several creators share one workflow 
                                   but differ only in which product they construct \\
            \hline
        \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
\end{table}

\subsection{Abstract Factory}
Abstract Factory groups creation methods for a whole product family behind one factory interface.
Swap the concrete factory and the client receives a matching set of products that work together, still typed only by abstract product interfaces.
Use it when ``theme'' or platform variants must stay consistent across several object kinds.

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{producing families 
                                             of related items} 
                                             without specifying their 
                                             concrete classes\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & declare 
                                           \highlightit{explicit interfaces} 
                                           for each distinct product 
                                           of the product family,
                                           and make all variants of 
                                           products follow those 
                                           interfaces; provide a factory 
                                           interface with one creation 
                                           method per product kind\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ client needs to work with 
                               various families of related objects, \\
                             & \quad without depending on the latter's 
                               concrete classes \\
                             & $\bullet$ a cluster of factory methods 
                               is blurring a class's main responsibility \\
                             & \quad and should be extracted\\
        \hline
        \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Builder}
Builder moves multi-step construction out of the product (and out of telescoping constructors) into dedicated builder objects.
Optional steps can be skipped; different builders can yield different representations from the same step sequence.
A Director is optional: it encodes recipes (step order) while the client still chooses which builder to use.

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{building complex 
                                             objects step-by-step}, producing 
                                             different types and 
                                             representations using 
                                             the same construction 
                                             steps\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & \highlightit{object construction code 
                                           is moved into a 
                                           Builder} class, 
                                           that builds the object through 
                                           steps that can be executed 
                                           in series and only if needed. 
                                           Several \texttt{Builder}s 
                                           can be implemented when 
                                           different ways of building 
                                           make sense. A \highlighttt{Director} 
                                           class can manage 
                                           the order of the building 
                                           steps. \\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ constructors became telescopic because 
                               of too many optional \\
                             & \quad parameters and need to be cleaned up\\
                             & $\bullet$ code needs to create 
                               different representations of some products \\
                             & $\bullet$ a \textit{composite} 
                               object construction needs to be managed 
                               step-by-step\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Prototype}
Prototype clones an existing instance instead of constructing from scratch through a type-specific API.
The clone operation lives on the object (or a shared prototype interface), so private state stays accessible without leaking into client code.
It shines when setup is expensive or when many variants differ only in initial configuration.

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{copying existing objects} 
                                             without implementing code 
                                             which depends on their 
                                             classes\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & a common interface is implemented 
                                           for all objects that need 
                                           to be cloned, and the actual 
                                           cloning procedure is delegated 
                                           to the objects themselves 
                                           in order to have access to 
                                           private and protected details 
                                           as well \\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ code shouldn't depend on the 
                                         concrete classes of the objects 
                                         being \\
                             & \quad copied\\
                             & $\bullet$ reducing the number of subclasses 
                                         that differ only in how they \\
                             & \quad initialize their respective objects\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Singleton}
Singleton guarantees at most one instance of a class and exposes a single global access point to it.
Hide the constructor and route creation through a static method that returns the cached instance on later calls.
Prefer it sparingly: shared mutable global state is hard to test and easy to overuse.

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{ensuring that a class has one instance only}, \\
                                           & while providing a global access point to that instance \\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & the \highlightit{constructor is made private} 
                                           to prevent calls from outside the class and a \highlightit{static creation method} is used in its place to create \\
                                         & the object and return it for successive calls\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ a class should have just a single instance available, \\
                             & \quad e.g.\ a shared database connection or configuration object \\
                             & $\bullet$ a stricter control over global variables is needed \\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Chain of Responsibility}
Pass a request along a chain of handlers so each can handle it or forward it. The client need not know which handler will process the request, and the chain order can be fixed at build time or rebuilt at runtime.
\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{pass a request along a 
                                             chain of handlers}, each 
                                             of which can either\\
                                           & manage the request or pass 
                                             it to the next handler 
                                             in the chain\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & each particular behavior 
                                           is implemented into a 
                                           stand-alone handler, \\
                                         & the handlers are linked 
                                           one to another in a chain 
                                           and the request,\\
                                         & along with the data needed, 
                                           is passed along the chain\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ different kinds of requests 
                               must be processed in different ways \\
                             & \quad and the request type is not known beforehand \\
                             & $\bullet$ the set or order of handlers 
                               can change at runtime\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Command}
Turn a request into a stand-alone object that stores the receiver and parameters. An invoker triggers \texttt{execute()} without knowing how the work is done, so commands can be queued, logged, or undone.
\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{turning a request into a 
                                             stand-alone object} containing 
                                             all information about it.\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & a request is turned into a 
                                           \highlightit{command object} 
                                           that stores the receiver and 
                                           the operation parameters; \\
                                         & an \highlightit{invoker} 
                                           triggers \texttt{execute()} 
                                           without knowing how the work 
                                           is done, so commands can be 
                                           queued, logged, or undone\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ there is the need to parametrize 
                               objects with operations\\
                             & $\bullet$ there is the need for 
                               reversible operations \\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Iterator}
Traverse a collection without exposing its underlying representation. Traversal logic lives in a separate iterator object; the collection only creates iterators that clients use through a common interface.
\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{traversing 
                                             elements of a collection 
                                             without exposing its underlying} \\
                                           & \highlightit{representation}\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & traversal logic is moved into a 
                                           separate \highlightit{iterator} 
                                           object that exposes 
                                           \texttt{next}/\texttt{hasNext} 
                                           (or equivalent); \\
                                         & the collection only creates 
                                           iterators, so clients walk 
                                           elements without knowing the 
                                           underlying structure\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ a collection has a complex 
                               structure that must stay hidden\\
                             & $\bullet$ reducing duplicated traversal 
                               code \\
                             & $\bullet$ traversing structures whose 
                               layout is unknown beforehand\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

                                             override specific steps\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & a base class defines a 
                                           \highlightit{template method}

                                           of steps; \\
                                         & invariant steps stay in the 
                                           base class, while 
                                           \highlightit{primitive/hook} 
                                           steps are abstract or 
                                           virtual so subclasses fill 
                                           them in via inheritance\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ client needs to extend only 
                                         specific steps of an algorithm \\

                                         classes containing almost 
                                         identical algorithms\\

                                           \highlightit{visitor} and 
                                           calls back 
                                           \texttt{visit(this)}; \\

                                           new visitor classes, while 
                                           the element hierarchy stays 
                                           closed to that change 
                                           (double dispatch)\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ performing an operation on all elements 
                               of a complex \\ 
                             & \quad data structure\\
                             & $\bullet$ cleaning up the business 
                               logic extracting other behaviors\\
                             & $\bullet$ separating behaviors that 
                               make sense only for specific classes\\

    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Adapter}
An \highlightit{Adapter} sits between a client that expects one interface and a
class that already works but speaks a different API.
It implements the target protocol and translates each call into the adaptee's
terms, so neither side needs to change.
Use it when you must reuse a third-party or legacy type without rewriting callers.

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & allowing \highlightit{objects with
        incompatible interfaces to collaborate}\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & an \highlightit{adapter}
                                           implements the interface
                                           clients expect and
                                           \highlightit{translates}
                                           calls to the adaptee's API; \\
                                         & clients depend only on the
                                           target interface, so the
                                           foreign class can be reused
                                           without changing either side\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ a class's interface is
                               incompatible with the rest of the
                               code\\
                             & $\bullet$ reusing subclasses that lack
                                         some common functionality
                                         that \\
                             & \quad cannot be added in the superclass\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

        \highlightbf{Client} & the business logic of the program\\

        \highlightbf{ClientInterface} & the protocol a class must follow

\subsection{Bridge}
\highlightit{Bridge} splits a fat hierarchy into two: an abstraction that
clients talk to, and an implementation hierarchy that does the low-level work.
Composition replaces a combinatorial inheritance tree, so each side can grow
on its own.
Swap the implementation at runtime when the platform or backend must change
without rewriting the abstraction.

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{splitting a large class
                                             or a set of related classes
                                             into two hierarchies}, an
                                             abstraction and an implementation,
                                             developed independently from each other\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & the \highlightit{abstraction}
                                           hierarchy holds a reference
                                           to an
                                           \highlightit{implementation}
                                           interface and delegates

                                         monolithic class that has

                             & \quad of some functionality \\
                             & $\bullet$ extending a class in several
                                         independent dimensions\\
                             & $\bullet$ switching implementation at
                                         runtime is a requirement\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

%%% %%% COMPOSITE %%% %%% %%%
\subsection{Composite}
\highlightit{Composite} builds tree structures where leaves and containers
share one component interface.
Clients call the same operations on a single node or on a whole subtree;
composites forward work to children.
Reach for it when the domain is naturally hierarchical (UI widgets, file
systems, expression trees).

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{composing objects
                                             into tree structures} in order
                                             to work with these structures
                                             as if they were individual
                                             objects\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & both leaf and composite
                                           objects implement the same
                                           component interface; leaves
                                           act as single entities while
                                           composites hold children,
                                           add/remove them, and delegate
                                           work downward\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ implementing a tree-like
                                         data structure \\
                             & $\bullet$ clients need to interact
                                         uniformly with simple and
                                         complex objects \\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

%%% %%% DECORATOR %%% %%% %%%
\subsection{Decorator}
\highlightit{Decorator} wraps an object in another object of the same
interface to add behavior without subclassing the core type.
Wrappers nest: each layer delegates to the inner component and can run
extra work before or after.
Prefer it when inheritance is blocked (\texttt{final}) or when combinations
of extras would explode a class hierarchy.

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{attach new behaviors to
                                             objects} by placing them inside
                                             special wrappers\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & objects are placed inside
                                           special wrapper objects
                                           that contain the new behaviors\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ extra behavior needs to be assigned to
                               objects at runtime\\
                             & $\bullet$ extending an object's behavior when
                               inheritance cannot be used \\
                             & \quad (for example when a class is
                               declared as \texttt{final})\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\subsection{Facade}
A \highlightit{Facade} hides a busy subsystem behind one simple entry point.
Clients call a few high-level methods; the facade knows which subsystem
objects to orchestrate.
Use it to layer a codebase or to stop every caller from wiring the same
complex dance.

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & providing users with a
                                             \highlightit{simplified
                                             interface to a complex set of
                                             classes}\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & a facade class provides a
                                           simple interface to a
                                           complex subsystem\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ a limited but straightforward
                               interface to a complex subsystem \\
                             & \quad is needed\\
                             & $\bullet$ a subsystem needs to be
                               structured into layers\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

%%% %%% FLYWEIGHT %%% %%% %%%
\subsection{Flyweight}
\highlightit{Flyweight} cuts memory by sharing the immutable, context-free
part of many similar objects (intrinsic state) and keeping the rest
(extrinsic) outside or passed in.
A factory hands out cached flyweights so identical intrinsic data is not
duplicated per instance.
Worth it when you create huge numbers of near-identical objects (glyphs,
particles, tiles).

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & reducing RAM consumption
                                             by \highlightit{sharing common parts
                                             of state} \highlightit{between multiple
                                             objects}, instead of keeping
                                             copies of the same data in
                                             each object\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & extract the
                                           \highlightit{intrinsic state}
                                           (invariant, context-independent,
                                           shareable) and pass the
                                           \highlightit{extrinsic state}
                                           (variant, context-dependent,
                                           unshareable) into operations\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ dealing with large numbers
                               of objects with repeated elements that \\
                             & \quad would use a large amount of memory if
                               stored individually\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

%%% %%% PROXY %%% %%% %%%
\subsection{Proxy}
A \highlightit{Proxy} stands in for a real service object under the same
interface, so clients need not know they are talking to a stand-in.
It can delay creation, check access, cache results, or log calls before
forwarding.
Choose the proxy flavour that matches the need (virtual, protection,
remote, smart reference).

\begin{table}[h!t]
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|l|X|}
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{What for}} & \highlightit{substitute
                                             or placeholder for another
                                             object}, \highlightit{controlling
                                             access} and allowing actions
                                             both before and after
                                             using the object\\
        \hline
        \multirow{1}{*}{\textbf{How to}} & a proxy class with the same
                                           interface as the original object
                                           accepts requests and forwards
                                           them to a real service object
                                           (often managing its lifecycle)\\
        \hline
        \textbf{When needed} & $\bullet$ lazy initialization\\
                             & $\bullet$ access control\\
                             & $\bullet$ local execution of a remote
                               service \\
                             & $\bullet$ logging the request history
                               through a proxy\\
                             & $\bullet$ caching request results,
                               especially when results are large\\
                             & $\bullet$ reference counting so a service
                               can be dismissed when no clients \\
                             & \quad remain\\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}
